Day 121: Free Rides to Harvard, MIT, Oxford: How I Never Paid a Penny for My Education

17 April 202517 เมษายน 20252025年4月17日

This entry is in English only.บันทึกนี้มีแต่ภาษาอังกฤษ此篇仅有英文。

People look at my resume — MIT, Oxford, Harvard — and they ask, “How the hell did you pull that off, Non?”

They expect me to say I had rich parents, a fat bank account, or that I was some child prodigy from Thailand who got a golden ticket from the government.

Nope. None of that.

My family wasn’t rich. I wasn’t born with a silver spoon or a brain that could solve quantum equations at age ten.

I was just a regular kid, playing basketball, skipping classes, and dreaming big in a small corner of Bangkok. So how did I end up at some of the world’s top schools without paying a single penny?

Buckle up, because it’s a story of grit, rejections, and a whole lot of library hours.

Back in the early 2000s, Thailand wasn’t the shiny metropolis it is now in 2025. Bangkok was a chaotic, backwater town — no mass transit, traffic jams everywhere, and if you wanted cool gadgets, you had to fly to Singapore or Hong Kong. I grew up in rural Thailand, went to local middle and high schools, and barely studied because I was too busy being a “lame kid” who thought basketball was life.

When I got into a normal college for architecture, it wasn’t exactly a victory parade.

But something shifted when I hit 22, fresh out of college.

I wanted more. I wanted to see the world, learn from the best, and not just be another cog in Thailand’s system.

Here’s the thing: I didn’t have money, and I wasn’t about to take out loans or beg my parents for cash. I also didn’t want a government scholarship. Sure, they’d pay for your PhD, but then you’re chained to a job back home for twice as long as you studied — or you pay back triple the cost. I saw friends get burned by that. One buddy, whom I knew through a physics camp years back, a quantum physics PhD from Harvard, came back and taught Physics 101—Newtonian stuff any undergrad could handle. What a waste! His cutting-edge lab days were over because Thailand didn’t need quantum theory. I wasn’t about to let that happen to me.

So, I got scrappy.

The internet was crap back then, so I’d visit university websites, request brochures by snail mail, and pore over them like they were treasure maps.

I dreamed of studying abroad, but every path seemed blocked by money or my mediocre high school grades. Then I realized something: my college years had given me an edge.

I’d spent five years in the library, not partying like my architecture classmates. I wasn’t the smartest, but I could outwork anyone. My English was decent—not great, but enough to give me a head start. And because I graduated top of my class (not hard in a third-tier college in a developing country that I went to), I didn’t pay a baht for my bachelor’s degree — even though it would still be quite cheap, like US$300/semester.

After graduating, I started teaching at a Thai university, but I kept my eyes on the horizon.

I didn’t want to be a professor living a cozy life in Bangkok. I wanted to learn from places where free speech and bold ideas drove innovation. So, I hit the ground running — attending study-abroad fairs, researching scholarships, applying to every grant I could find.

Rejection letters piled up like junk mail.

England, Netherlands—some schools accepted me, but they wanted tuition I couldn’t afford.

Loans? No way. With my measly teaching salary, I’d be drowning in debt.

But I kept going, like a fisherman fixing his nets when the sea’s too rough.

I studied harder, improved my English, and prepped for the GRE like my life depended on it.

Rejections didn’t faze me. I’d laugh at them, toss them aside, and keep applying. By the 20th letter, I was numb to failure. Then, one day, a package arrived: the Fulbright Scholarship. The holy grail of grants—fully funded, two years in the U.S., no strings attached. I’d applied the year before and bombed, but this time, I nailed the interview. I walked in, criticized Thai politics, and laid it all bare. They saw me for who I was: a kid from nowhere with a fire to learn.

Fulbright changed everything.

Suddenly, I had the funds, but I needed a school. I applied to a few, and MIT said yes. MIT!

They covered half my tuition, Fulbright and the Asian Cultural Council handled the rest, and I even freed up some grant money for other students. Fun fact: I was a Fulbrighter the same year as Rishi Sunak, who later became UK prime minister. We probably crossed paths at some fancy Fulbright event, but I didn’t notice him —he was just another shy grad student then.

MIT was just the start. I wanted more—a master’s, a PhD. But the game got harder. Once you’re in the U.S., you’re no longer the “underprivileged kid from a developing country.” My Thai accent was thick, my English writing was a mess, and GREs and TOEFLs were brutal. Rejections poured in again—Stanford, MIT (yep, they rejected me for a second degree). But I kept at it, fishing for grants like my life depended on it.

After maybe 50 rejections, I landed the Harvard-Yenching Institute Scholarship. It was a lifeline for Asian Studies research, and because I was a faculty member in Thailand with solid recommendations, I squeaked through.

The catch?

None actually. Harvard-Yenching let me start at Oxford for a Master of Philosophy in Modern Chinese Studies.

Two years later, I applied to Harvard’s Anthropology PhD program—one of the toughest, with a 1% acceptance rate. Stanford rejected me. MIT rejected me. But Harvard said yes. I’d done it again—no debt, no rich parents, just a kid who wouldn’t quit.

Looking back, I realize my journey wasn’t about being rich or a genius. It was about finding the right opportunities and outlasting the rejections. In 2025, with information at our fingertips, it’s easier to track down scholarships, but the mindset hasn’t changed. You’ve got to dig, persist, and believe you’re worth it.

Three Key Takeaways

  1. Research Like Your Future Depends on It
    I didn’t know scholarships like Fulbright or Harvard-Yenching existed until I started digging. Back then, I scoured university websites and begged for brochures. Today, you’ve got Google—use it. Look for keywords like “merit-based scholarships” or “grants for developing countries.” There’s money out there for people like you, but you have to find it. The more you learn, the more doors open.
  2. Embrace Rejection as Part of the Game
    I got rejected so many times—17 before Fulbright, 50 before Harvard-Yenching. Each “no” was a punch, but I laughed it off and kept going. Rejections aren’t personal; they’re just noise. If you let them stop you, you’ll never find the “yes” waiting at the end. Keep fixing your net, and the tide will turn.
  3. Teach Yourself to Win
    I wasn’t born smart. High school me was a slacker. But college me spent thousands of hours in the library, reading, learning, and building skills. I taught myself English, prepped for exams, and outworked everyone. You don’t need a fancy school to start—you just need a fire to learn and the discipline to follow through.

In the end, I never paid a penny because I believed I could find a way. If a regular kid from rural Thailand can do it, so can you. Stop thinking you need money or a genius IQ. Start researching, keep pushing, and bet on yourself. The world’s waiting.
