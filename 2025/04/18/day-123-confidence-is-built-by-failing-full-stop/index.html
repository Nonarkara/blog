Day 123: Confidence Is Built by Failing. Full Stop.

18 April 202518 เมษายน 20252025年4月18日

This entry is in English only.บันทึกนี้มีแต่ภาษาอังกฤษ此篇仅有英文。

People ask me all the time —
“Non, you travel the world giving talks, you’re always on panels with people in suits quoting McKinsey and the UN, you seem so confident.”

“How did you get there? Did you read some life-changing book? Did you practice those TED-style hand gestures in the mirror?”

Nope.

Here’s my secret: I failed my way into it.

Let me tell you something people don’t say enough. We live in a world obsessed with confidence. Fake it till you make it. Hustle culture. “Believe in yourself” memes on Instagram. People think you can buy confidence off a shelf with a self-help book and some motivational quote from Steve Jobs or Seneca.

But here’s the truth — you don’t think yourself into confidence.


You do.


And you fail.


A lot.


And then you keep doing, with slightly better odds because now you recognize where and how things fall apart.

Public Speaking: My Favorite Failure

If you met me five years ago and told me I’d be speaking on stages in front of thousands, traveling 180,000 km a year, doing global talks, keynotes, opening ceremonies, moderating international panels — I’d laugh in your face.

I hated public speaking.

And I sucked at it.

I used to think public speaking was about showing how smart I was. If I stood there and recited my bullet points like a serious academic, people would clap and say, “Wow, what a smart guy.” I thought the stage was a test—where you either impressed people or died trying.

So the first time I got up there, I froze.

Completely blanked.

The content was weak. I didn’t know it well enough. Couldn’t explain it because I never really understood it. I was memorizing words I barely believed in. And when the questions came? I stumbled through half-answers, hoping no one noticed.

I walked off that stage like a dead man.

But — something interesting happened.

I wasn’t angry, or embarrassed, or broken.

I was curious.

Why did I crash? What went wrong? Where was the glitch?

The Neuroscience of Failing (Why Your Brain Needs It)

Here’s what I learned later digging into neuroscience:

Your brain learns best when it makes mistakes — with consequences, but not ones that destroy you. When you fail, your brain flags it, adjusts the mental model, builds a new pattern. It says “Okay, let’s not step there again.”

That’s how real confidence is built.

Not from TED Talks.

Not from pep talks.

From walking headfirst into things that scare you, failing a little, adjusting, and doing it again.

Calculated risk is key here.

You don’t want to fail so bad it ruins you.

You want small, frequent, annoying failures that slowly make you better.

Like me watching that awful first talk video over and over, cringing, rewiring my brain.

What I Actually Learned About Speaking

I learned three huge things from that trainwreck:

  1. Know Your Damn Material
    Don’t memorize words. Understand ideas. Deeply. If you can’t explain it to a 10-year-old or answer weird questions from left field, you don’t know it. Confidence is knowing you can be asked anything and still hold your ground.
  2. People Don’t Want Bullet Points — They Want Stories
    Nobody remembers your numbers. Nobody quotes your definitions. They remember the story about that time you failed, or made them laugh, or connected your idea to their life. Communication isn’t information transfer. It’s emotional transfer.
  3. If You Don’t Care, They Won’t Either
    Delivery is everything. If you’re up there just to read a script or tick a box, stay home. People smell that fear and disinterest a mile away. You need to care so much it leaks into your voice, your eyes, your posture. If you fake it, they’ll fake their applause too.

Failure Is the Training Ground for Experts

People don’t realize this — expertise isn’t born from talent. It’s born from patterns. And patterns are born from failure.

The more you fail, the more patterns you see.

The more patterns you see, the better decisions you make.

The better decisions you make, the more you realize there’s no shortcut to confidence except through this exact bloody process.

That’s why the guys who stay safe, who never take risks, who avoid the stage, or avoid applying for that scholarship, or avoid the new job because “I’m not ready” — they stay where they are. Because the pattern never gets written.

The Final Takeaway

Confidence comes from battle scars. Not pep talks.

You want to get better? Pick something you’re afraid of.

Do it.

Fail.

Watch yourself fail.

Analyze why it happened.

Fix your net.

Try again.

That’s it. That’s the formula.

Books won’t save you.
Quotes won’t toughen you.
You don’t need to be fearless.
You need to be a realist with guts.

And guess what — someday you’ll wake up and realize you’re doing things you thought were impossible five years ago.

That’s me now.

It could be you next.
